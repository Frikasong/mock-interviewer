# Step 2 — Self research

Goal: turn "who you are" into structured material an AI can quote, rather than a résumé PDF.

Working assumption: **everybody has stories worth telling; most people just haven't found theirs yet.** When someone says they have no highlights, it's usually because they did something hard well enough that it stopped looking hard. Ask about the process, the friction, and what would have happened if they hadn't acted — the story is in there.

---

## 2.1 Interrogate the résumé, line by line

Go through it against the JD and question **every line**, not just the impressive ones. The reason is practical: you can't predict which experience the interviewer will pick up on, and full coverage is the only source of fluency under pressure.

```
Read my résumé as an interviewer would, against this job description, and question
my experience line by line. Two questions per line minimum: one on detail (what
exactly did I do, what was the outcome) and one on judgment (why that approach,
what was the alternative). Output a document I can answer in directly.
```

Hand the list to the user. **Don't fill it in for them.** When answers come back vague, push:

- "The team did X" → which part was yours?
- "It went well" → is there a number? If not, what changed afterwards?
- "It was a bit difficult" → difficult how? Who objected? What did you do about it?
- "I coordinated it" → did coordination ever fail? How did that end?

Tell them plainly that this step deserves a few uninterrupted hours going back over every line of the résumé. It has the highest return of anything in the process.

## 2.2 STAR cards

Ten to fifteen of them, one file each, numbered S01, S02… (the numbers exist so the question bank can reference them).

The proportions are the point:

- **Situation** — one or two sentences. Background is where beginners lose their time budget.
- **Task** — the problem, and *your role in it*.
- **Action** — more than half the card. The subject is "I". If a card's action section is all "we", it fails; send it back.
- **Result** — a number where one exists. Where none exists: what changed, what was decided, what got adopted afterwards.

Template in `assets/story-card.md`.

```
Here is my résumé and supplementary notes. Turn them into 10-15 standalone STAR
story cards. Each card: title / situation / task / action / quantified result /
which common questions this story answers / a conclusion-first opening line.
Output markdown, one story per file. Use only facts I have supplied — do not add
or infer details.
```

**One story answers several questions**, and the reverse. A good bank isn't fifteen stories for fifteen questions; it's twelve stories covering forty. Tag each card with the types it covers: conflict, failure, leadership, prioritisation, cross-cultural, fast learning, catching an error, persuading someone.

Coverage check: if no card can answer "tell me about a time you failed," that's a gap — go dig. It's near-universal, and it's the question where improvisation is most obvious.

## 2.3 Selling-point matrix

One table. Pull the employer's three most-valued traits out of the Step 1 JD teardown, then fill in evidence.

| They value | My evidence | One-line version |
|---|---|---|
| Independent judgment | S02, S09 | I form a view first, then take it somewhere it can be knocked down |
| Written clarity | S04, S11 | … |
| Working under load | S06 | … |

Each trait needs at least one story. A trait with no evidence is a preparation gap, not a modesty problem.

## 2.4 Self-introduction

Structure: who I am now → the line that connects the past → why I'm sitting here.

- Write a 30-second version (opening) and a 90-second version ("walk me through your résumé")
- Don't recite the timeline; explain why the line leads to this role
- End on them, not on you
- Read it aloud against a timer. Over time, cut background — never cut the conclusion.

## 2.5 Weaknesses and other set pieces

Three parts, written in advance rather than improvised:

1. **Candid** — something real that doesn't sit on top of the role's core competency
2. **What's already being done** — a specific action with a timeframe
3. **Back to the role** — why it doesn't compromise the work, or why this role helps fix it

Avoid "I'm a perfectionist" and "I work too hard." Interviewers hear them daily and they read as evasion.

Prepare the other predictable set pieces the same way: why you left, any gap in the timeline, where else you're interviewing, salary expectations, and questions for them. That last one needs three prepared questions, at least one of which shows you've read their annual report or strategy document.
